Industrial Robotics and Factory Automation

An industrial robot is an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose
manipulator that is programmable in three or more axes. Reprogrammability separates
a true robot from fixed automation such as a cam driven machine, and it is what
allows one robot cell to build one product this month and a different product the
next.

The mechanical structure of a robot is a chain of rigid links connected by joints.
Revolute joints rotate and prismatic joints slide, and the number of independent
joints gives the number of degrees of freedom. Six degrees of freedom are needed to
place the end effector at an arbitrary position and orientation in space. Common
configurations include the articulated arm, which offers the largest dexterous
workspace, the SCARA arm used for assembly and pick and place, the delta parallel
robot used for high speed packaging, and the Cartesian gantry used for large
working volumes.

Kinematics relates joint angles to end effector position. Forward kinematics
computes the tool position from known joint angles and has a unique solution.
Inverse kinematics computes the joint angles required to reach a desired tool
position and may have several solutions, one solution, or none if the point lies
outside the workspace. The Denavit Hartenberg convention assigns a coordinate frame
to each link so the transformation between links is described by four parameters.
Singular configurations, where two joint axes become collinear, cause the robot to
lose a degree of freedom and must be avoided in trajectory planning.

Actuation is dominated by alternating current servo motors with integrated encoders
and harmonic drive gearboxes that provide high reduction ratios with very low
backlash. Hydraulic actuation is still used for very heavy payloads and pneumatic
actuation for simple two position grippers. The end effector may be a parallel jaw
gripper, a vacuum cup array, a welding torch, a paint atomiser or a dispensing
nozzle.

Sensing gives the robot awareness of its environment. Internal sensors measure joint
position, velocity and motor current. External sensors include machine vision
cameras for part location and inspection, force and torque sensors at the wrist for
assembly tasks such as inserting a shaft into a bearing, proximity sensors, and
laser profile scanners for weld seam tracking. Machine vision has advanced rapidly
because convolutional neural networks can classify defects that were previously
detectable only by a trained human inspector.

Control architecture is hierarchical. A current loop runs at tens of kilohertz, a
velocity loop above it, and a position loop above that. The trajectory generator
produces a smooth path with controlled acceleration so the robot does not excite
structural vibration. Modern controllers add feedforward compensation for gravity,
friction and inertia coupling, and impedance control that lets the robot behave
like a programmable spring when it contacts the workpiece.

Collaborative robots, called cobots, share a workspace with human operators without
a safety fence. They achieve this through low mass and rounded geometry, torque
sensing in every joint to detect contact, speed and separation monitoring using
laser scanners, and a certified safety controller that limits force and power. The
standard ISO 10218 and the technical specification ISO TS 15066 define permissible
contact forces for each part of the human body.

Programming methods have broadened. Teach pendant programming records points as the
operator moves the robot manually. Offline programming builds and simulates the
whole cell in a computer aided design environment, avoiding production stoppage.
Lead through programming lets the operator guide a cobot arm by hand. Learning from
demonstration and reinforcement learning are increasingly used for tasks that are
hard to specify explicitly, such as bin picking of randomly oriented parts.

Industrial robots are now embedded in Industry 4.0, where machines, products and
enterprise software exchange data continuously. A robot cell publishes cycle time,
torque and vibration data to a cloud platform where predictive maintenance models
estimate remaining gearbox life. Digital twins mirror the physical cell in software
so a process change can be validated virtually before deployment. Reported effects
include thirty to fifty percent higher throughput and large reductions in scrap and
in workplace injuries.
