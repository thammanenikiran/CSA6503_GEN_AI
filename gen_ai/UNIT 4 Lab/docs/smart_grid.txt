Smart Grid Technology in Modern Power Systems

The traditional electrical grid was designed for a one way flow of power, from
large central generating stations through high voltage transmission lines, down
through distribution substations, and finally to the consumer. Control was
centralised, measurement was sparse, and faults were often detected only when a
customer telephoned the utility to report an outage. Rising demand, the entry of
renewable generation and the need for higher reliability have made this model
inadequate.

A smart grid is an electrical network that uses digital communication, sensing and
automatic control to monitor and manage the transport of electricity from all
generation sources to meet the varying demand of end users. The essential
difference from the conventional grid is the two way flow of both power and
information. A rooftop solar installation can export surplus energy back into the
distribution feeder, and the utility can in turn send price signals or control
commands to the consumer premises.

The architecture of a smart grid is described in layers. The physical layer
contains generators, transformers, transmission lines, circuit breakers and loads.
The sensing layer consists of smart meters at consumer premises, phasor
measurement units on transmission lines and intelligent electronic devices inside
substations. Phasor measurement units sample voltage and current waveforms up to
sixty times per second and time stamp each sample using a global positioning
system clock, which allows the state of the entire grid to be observed in real
time. The communication layer carries this data over fibre optic links, power line
carrier communication, cellular networks and radio mesh networks. The application
layer runs supervisory control and data acquisition systems, energy management
systems, distribution management systems and outage management systems.

Advanced metering infrastructure is often the first component a utility deploys. A
smart meter records consumption every fifteen or thirty minutes and transmits the
readings automatically. This eliminates manual meter reading, enables accurate time
of use tariffs, and allows the utility to detect theft by comparing energy injected
into a feeder with the sum of energy recorded by all meters on that feeder.
Consumers benefit because they can see their own consumption pattern and shift
flexible loads such as water heaters, washing machines and electric vehicle
charging to off peak hours when tariffs are lower.

Integration of renewable energy is the most demanding challenge the smart grid is
designed to solve. Solar and wind generation are intermittent and are not
dispatchable in the way a coal or gas plant is. Cloud cover can reduce the output
of a solar farm by seventy percent within a few minutes. Because the grid must
balance generation and demand instantaneously, a high share of renewable generation
increases the need for fast acting reserves. Smart grids address this using
forecasting algorithms based on satellite imagery and machine learning, battery
energy storage systems that inject or absorb power within milliseconds, and demand
response programmes in which large consumers reduce load on request.

Self healing is another defining capability. In a conventional distribution network
a fault causes an outage for every customer downstream of the protective device
that operates, and restoration requires a crew to patrol the line. In a self
healing network, fault passage indicators and automatic sectionalising switches
identify the faulted section within seconds, isolate it, and reconfigure the
network so that healthy sections are supplied from an alternative feeder. Typical
restoration times fall from hours to under a minute.

Cyber security is a serious concern because the connectivity that makes the grid
intelligent also creates an attack surface. A compromised smart meter network could
be used to issue mass disconnect commands, and a compromised substation controller
could open circuit breakers and cause cascading failure. Defensive measures include
strong mutual authentication between devices, encryption of all control traffic,
segmentation of the operational technology network from the corporate information
technology network, intrusion detection tuned to industrial protocols, and signed
firmware update procedures.

Economic and regulatory factors decide how quickly smart grids are adopted. The
capital cost of metering infrastructure, communication networks and automation
equipment is very high, and many utilities operate under tariffs that do not reward
efficiency improvements. Interoperability is also incomplete because equipment from
different vendors implements different communication standards. Standards such as
IEC 61850 for substation automation, IEEE 2030 for interoperability and the common
information model of IEC 61970 are steadily reducing this fragmentation.

Looking ahead, the distribution network is expected to evolve into interconnected
microgrids, each able to operate independently when the main grid fails. Electric
vehicles will act as distributed storage through vehicle to grid technology.
Artificial intelligence will move from forecasting into autonomous control,
predicting equipment failure from vibration and thermal signatures and scheduling
maintenance before a fault occurs. The result is a grid that is cleaner, more
reliable, more efficient and far more observable than the one it replaces.
